## Core promise (cornerstone — Phill verbatim, 2026-07-08)

> "The more you learn, the more potential to earn."

Every asset descends from this. Note the register: **capability and potential — never an
income promise.** This phrasing is the legal line as well as the brand line.

## The axis: "What's in it for me?"

Phill's entire commercial worldview routes through the contractor's question — his words:

> "The biggest question most contractors ask when they join an association is, what's in it
> for me… they want the value of that dollar — for every dollar that they spend they should
> perceive that they're getting ten dollars back."

**Rule:** every piece of copy must answer *what's in it for the operator* within the first
two sentences — in concrete capability, time, standing, or knowledge. If a draft leads with
the brand instead of the operator, it's off-voice.

## Voice pillars (observed, not invented)

1. **Trade-grounded first person.** He earns the right to speak from lived experience:
   "I come from the cleaning industry background… the specialised cleaning and restoration
   industry, which I've been a part of." Copy claims standing the same way — from the floor,
   not the boardroom.
2. **Mechanism-level critique, never vague complaint.** His RIA critique names the machine:
   volunteers, 10–15 unpaid hours a week, so "they don't have time to think… and the members
   are left without any understanding." When copy contrasts with the status quo, it explains
   *why* the old way fails, specifically — and never names a competitor with a claim we
   can't substantiate.
3. **Data is the value.** "What we're after is data… real, actual, reliable data… it's not
   anybody else's data, it's our data." Proof, numbers and sources aren't compliance
   overhead in this voice — they ARE the pitch.
4. **Elevate the operator.** The point is the operator's "own value and presence into the
   community" — skin in the game, listened to, heard. Copy positions the reader as the one
   being built up, never talked down to.
5. **Educator identity.** "The industry educator, the industry experts, the knowledge base."
   Confident teaching register: explain, don't hype.
6. **Ambition without corporate varnish.** "How to build an empire" — said plainly. Big
   goals in small words.

## Cadence rules (spoken → written translation)

- **Short declaratives, then a rolling list.** He stacks: "We'll need it for RestoreAssist.
  We'll need it for Disaster Recovery. We need it for the NRPG." Use deliberate repetition
  for emphasis — but at most once per asset.
- **Question-then-answer.** He poses the reader's own question ("What's in it for me?") and
  answers it straight. Use as a section device.
- **Plain connectives.** "So", "Now", "And the reason I do that is…" are on-voice. Avoid
  "Moreover", "Furthermore", "Leverage", "Unlock".
- **Concrete over abstract, number over adjective.** "Up to 100 employees or $10 to $15
  million" — he defines his terms with numbers. Matches the skill's specificity law.
- **Direct address.** "You" is the subject of the benefit sentences; "we/I" only for
  standing and commitments.

## Say / never say

| On-voice | Off-voice (never) |
|---|---|
| operator, contractor, technician, on site, scope of works | end-user, stakeholder, best-in-class, world-class |
| "here's what that means for you" | "empowering synergies", "revolutionise" |
| "what's in it for you? This." | hype adjectives without a number attached |
| Australian English (mould, odour, licence, -ise) | US spelling (a defect, per CARSI standard) |
| capability, skills, knowledge, standing, potential | income promises, "earn $X", projected/typical earnings (BANNED — grill Q5) |
